    Is the edge real?

    Could the lead over SPY be luck? Based on quarterly returns minus SPY's. See Learn → p-value and Confidence interval.

    Is the edge steady?

    The strategy's 1-year return minus SPY's, for every 1-year window. Above 0 = it beat SPY over that year. The shaded stretch is the worst year. See Learn → Tracking error and Information ratio.

      Robustness

      Same strategy, slightly different rules. Turnover = share of the portfolio replaced per rebalance; cost drag = yearly return lost to trading costs. If results only look good under the baseline settings, the edge is probably luck. Healthy = every row beats the S&P 500 by a similar margin.

      Start-date luck

      The same strategy, started on 12 different dates (each quarterly calendar, starting 0, 3, 6 or 9 months later). Each dot is one backtest; the big one is the headline. A headline far above the rest would mean a lucky start. See Learn → Start-date luck.

      How long does a pick's edge last?

      Every past rebalance's picks vs the average stock, measured 1 to 12 months later. The strategy holds for 3 months (outlined bar). See Learn → Signal decay.

      Does a top-5 streak mean anything?

      Every past rebalance's picks, split in two: stayers (also picked the quarter before) and new picks. Excess = the pick's next-quarter return minus the average stock's. See Learn → Streak effect.

      What the numbers mean

      Every term on this site, in plain English, with a tiny example. Your practice progress →
      Factor
      A single stat used to rank stocks (e.g. revenue growth).
      Example: MU's revenue grew 40% vs a year ago — that's its value for the revenue-growth factor.
      Multiple testing / data mining
      Try enough versions of a strategy and one will look great by pure luck. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado) raises the bar: it estimates the best Sharpe you'd get from that many no-skill tries, then asks how likely our Sharpe beats that, adjusting for fat tails.
      Example: Flip 20 coins 10 times each. The "best" coin probably landed heads 8+ times, but it isn't a better coin. Same with the best of 20 backtests.
      P/S vs its own history
      Price-to-sales (market value ÷ last 12 months of sales), ranked against the same stock's weekly P/S over the past 5 years. 0th percentile = the cheapest it has been, 100th = the priciest. Comparing a stock with its own past avoids comparing a chip designer with a memory maker. Shown for context only: it is not part of the score.
      Example: MU at 2.0× sales, when its weekly P/S over 5 years ranged from 1.5× to 6×, sits near the 10th percentile: "near its cheapest". For cyclicals that can be a trap, because sales may be about to fall.
      Tracking error
      How far a strategy typically wanders from its benchmark in a year: the standard deviation of its daily return minus SPY's daily return, scaled to a year (× √252). Low = it behaves a lot like SPY. High = it can beat or trail SPY by a lot.
      Example: Tracking error 15% means a year 15 points ahead of or behind SPY is ordinary for this strategy, not a sign it broke.
      Information ratio
      Edge per unit of wandering: the average yearly lead over SPY ÷ tracking error. It's a Sharpe ratio measured against SPY instead of cash. Above 0.5 is good for a stock picker; above 1 is rare and worth double-checking.
      Example: Beating SPY by 6% a year with 12% tracking error gives IR 0.5. Beating it by 6% with 30% tracking error gives 0.2: same average, much bumpier ride.
      Bootstrap
      A way to see a range instead of one number: take the real history, cut it into pieces (here, 1-year blocks of quarters), and stitch random pieces back together thousands of times. Each stitched path is a "could have happened" version of the past. It can't invent new disasters the history never had, so treat the worst cases as optimistic.
      Example: 5,000 replays of 5 years: in 8 of 10, $1,000 ended between $1,100 and $5,900. The single backtest is just one of those paths.
      Underwater / recovery time
      "Underwater" means below your last high. Max drawdown says how deep the worst drop was; recovery time says how long it took to get back to the old high. A −30% drop that recovers in 4 months feels very different from one that takes 3 years, even though the drawdown number is the same.
      Example: $10,000 grows to $15,000 (the high), falls to $9,000 (−40%), and is back to $15,000 two years after the high: 2 years underwater.
      Up/down capture
      Take only the months the benchmark went up: what share of its average gain did the strategy get? That's up-capture. Do the same for down months: down-capture. Over 100% up and under 100% down means more of the gains and less of the losses. Over 100% on both is just a more volatile version of the benchmark.
      Example: SOXX averages +6% in its up months and the strategy +6.5%: up-capture 108%. In down months SOXX averages −6% and the strategy −5%: down-capture 83%.
      Streak effect
      The idea that a stock which keeps showing up in the top picks is a better bet than one that just arrived. It's testable: compare how stayers and new picks did over the next quarter, quarter by quarter. If the gap is small next to how much it jumps around (t below 2), the streak is a nice story, not an edge.
      Example: Stayers +1.0% a quarter vs new picks +1.6%, t −0.4: no reason to trust a stayer more.
      Signal decay
      How fast a stock-picking signal stops working. A pick made today might beat the average stock over the next 3 months, then lose that lead (or reverse) by month 12. If the edge fades fast, you rebalance often; if it lasts, you can trade less and save costs.
      Example: Picks are +1.2% ahead after 3 months but −6% behind after 12: hold for about a quarter, then re-pick.
      Start-date luck
      A backtest's result depends a little on the exact day it starts: a start just before a crash looks bad, one just after looks great. Re-running from many starts shows how much of the headline is the strategy and how much is the calendar. Trust the middle of the range, not the best dot.
      Example: If 12 starts give 17% to 24% a year and the headline says 24%, expect something nearer 20%.
      Relative strength
      A group's (or stock's) return minus a benchmark's over the same period. +5% vs SOXX over 3 months means it beat the semis ETF by 5 points. Leadership tends to persist for a while (momentum), but rotates over a cycle.
      Example: Equipment +12%, SOXX +7% → relative strength +5%.
      Breadth
      How many members of a group are participating: here, the share of its stocks trading above their own 50-day average. A rally where 90% of stocks are up is sturdier than one carried by a single big name.
      Example: 5 of 6 Equipment stocks above their 50-day average → breadth 83%.
      p-value
      If the strategy had no real edge, how often would plain luck give a result at least this good? Small (under 0.05) means luck is an unlikely explanation. It is not the chance the strategy works.
      Example: p = 0.20 means a no-skill strategy would look this good 1 time in 5. Not convincing.
      Confidence interval
      A range of plausible values for the true edge, given how noisy the data is. Here: a 90% range from resampling the quarters (bootstrap). If the whole range is above 0, the edge is probably real. If it straddles 0, you can't tell yet.
      Example: "−4% to +18% per year" means the data fits anything from a small loss to a big win. You need more quarters.
      Inverse-volatility weighting
      Instead of equal dollars in each pick, put more money in calmer stocks and less in jumpy ones: weight ∝ 1 ÷ volatility (last ~3 months). Each pick then adds a similar amount of day-to-day swing. It usually smooths the ride; whether it helps returns depends on the period (see the "Inverse-vol weights" row in the robustness table).
      Example: Stock A swings 30% a year, stock B 60%. Equal: 50/50. Calmer-weighted: A gets 2/3, B gets 1/3, because B is twice as jumpy.
      Earnings reaction
      How much a stock moved on the first trading day after each of its last 8 quarterly SEC filings, compared with a normal day. "2.3× a normal day" means earnings days are much bumpier than usual. We use the filing date as a stand-in for the earnings announcement, which usually lands the same day or a bit earlier.
      Example: If a stock usually moves ±2% a day but ±7% after earnings, buying the day before a report is partly a bet on that ±7% coin-flip. Some people wait until after the report.
      σ (z-move)
      How unusual a price move is for that stock: the move divided by the stock's typical daily move over the past year. 1σ is a normal day; 3σ+ happens only a few times a year.
      Example: NVDA usually moves about 3% a day, so a −9% day is a 3σ move. For a calm stock that moves 1% a day, −3% would already be 3σ.
      Beta
      How much a stock or portfolio tends to move when the market moves 1%. Beta 1.5 means it usually moves about 1.5% when the S&P 500 moves 1%, both up and down.
      Example: The market falls 10%. A portfolio with beta 1.3 would typically fall about 13%.
      Correlation
      How much two stocks move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (in lockstep). High correlation between your holdings means less real diversification.
      Example: AMAT and LRCX both sell chip-making equipment, so their correlation is often 0.8+. When one has a bad day, the other usually does too.
      VaR (value at risk)
      A "bad day" size. 95% one-day VaR is the loss you'd expect to exceed on about 1 trading day in 20, based on recent history. It is NOT the worst case: crashes can be much bigger.
      Example: VaR of 2.4% on $1,000 means about one day a month you'd lose $24 or more.
      Effective bets
      How many truly independent positions your portfolio acts like. Five stocks that always move together act like ~1 bet. Five unrelated stocks act like 5.
      Example: 5 chip picks with "2.0 effective bets" diversify about as much as 2 unrelated stocks.
      Out-of-sample
      Testing a rule on data it was NOT built from. If you tune a strategy on 2012–2019, then 2020 onward is out-of-sample. Results there are the honest ones; in-sample results are usually too good.
      Example: A student who memorized last year's exam (in-sample) scores 95. On this year's new exam (out-of-sample) they score 70. The 70 is their real skill.
      Walk-forward
      Re-choosing the rules at each step using only the data available up to that day, then testing on what came next. It's the closest a backtest gets to how you'd really have used it.
      Example: In June 2018 the model weights each stat by how well it worked from mid-2015 to mid-2018, and nothing later.
      z-score
      How far a stock is from the group average, measured in standard deviations. 0 = average, +1 = clearly above, −1 = clearly below.
      Example: Average growth is 10% with a spread of 15%. A stock growing 40% has z = (40−10)/15 = +2.
      Composite score
      The weighted average of a stock's z-scores across all factors. Higher = better overall.
      Example: z-scores of +2, 0 and −1 with equal weights → score ≈ +0.33.
      Point-in-time
      Only using information that was actually public on that date — the backtest can't peek at future reports.
      Example: A quarter's numbers filed Aug 28 only count from Aug 28 on, even though the quarter ended in July.
      Backtest
      Replaying the strategy on past data to see how it would have done. Useful for checking ideas, but NOT proof.
      Example: “If I'd bought the top 5 every quarter since 2012…”
      Paper portfolio
      Fake-money trades logged before the results are known. The honest test of whether the model works.
      Example: Picks recorded Dec 1 are compared with what the stocks actually do through Mar 1.
      CAGR
      Compound annual growth rate — the steady yearly return that would give the same total result.
      Example: $1 → $2 over 5 years is a CAGR of about 15%/year.
      Volatility
      How much returns bounce around, as a yearly %. Higher = bumpier ride.
      Example: 30% volatility means a typical year could easily land ±30% from average.
      Sharpe ratio
      Return per unit of volatility. Above 1 is good; the S&P 500 is usually around 0.5–0.8.
      Example: 15% return with 15% volatility → Sharpe ≈ 1.0.
      Max drawdown
      The worst peak-to-bottom drop. Tells you how much pain you'd have sat through.
      Example: $100 → $140 → $84 is a −40% drawdown (from the $140 peak).
      IC (information coefficient)
      Rank correlation between a factor and next quarter's returns. 0 = useless, 0.05+ = useful, 0.10+ = strong.
      Example: If the stocks ranked highest by a factor tend to rise most, IC is positive.
      t-stat
      How many standard errors an average (an IC, or the edge over SPY) is away from zero: how confident we are it isn't luck. Above ~2 = probably real.
      Example: IC averaging 0.06 with t = 3.1 over 50 quarters is unlikely to be chance.
      Hit rate
      % of periods where the thing worked (IC above zero, or strategy beat SPY).
      Example: Beat SPY in 34 of 58 quarters → 59% hit rate.
      Robustness
      Whether results survive small rule changes. If only one exact setting works, it was probably luck.
      Example: Holding 5 stocks returns 20%/yr but holding 8 returns 3% → fragile.
      Overfitting
      Tuning rules until the past looks great. The model memorizes noise and fails on new data.
      Example: Trying 200 weight combos and keeping the best backtest is overfitting.
      Survivorship bias
      Only testing on companies that still exist today, which makes the past look better than it was.
      Example: A 2012 investor might have bought a chip stock that later went bust — our list doesn't include it.
      Sales yield / P/S
      Sales yield = yearly revenue ÷ company value. P/S (price-to-sales) is its inverse. Higher yield = cheaper.
      Example: Revenue $10B, market cap $50B → sales yield 20%, P/S 5×.
      Days of inventory
      How many days of sales are sitting in the warehouse. Rising fast is a classic chip-downturn warning.
      Example: $2B inventory with $8B quarterly cost of goods → about 23 days.
      TTM
      Trailing twelve months — the last four quarters added together. Smooths out seasonality.
      Example: TTM revenue = the latest four quarters' revenue summed.
      Turnover
      The share of the portfolio replaced at each rebalance. More turnover = more trading costs and taxes.
      Example: Swapping 2 of 5 stocks = 40% turnover.
      Cost drag
      Yearly return lost to trading costs. Small per trade, but it compounds.
      Example: 40% turnover × 4 rebalances × 2 sides × 0.10% ≈ 0.3% a year.
      Attribution / contribution
      How much of the portfolio's return came from each stock: its weight × its return.
      Example: A 20% position that rose 15% contributed 3 percentage points.
      P&L (profit and loss)
      How much money you've made or lost. Realized = locked in when you sold; unrealized = on shares you still own, which can still change.
      Example: Buy 10 at $50, sell 4 at $60 (+$40 realized), the rest now $55 (+$30 unrealized): total P&L +$70.
      Basis points (bps)
      1/100th of a percent. Used for trading costs.
      Example: 10 bps = 0.10% — costs $1 on a $1,000 trade.

      How it works

      5 quick stops: where to look first.

      Every weekday evening a GitHub Action downloads fresh SEC filings and prices, re-scores ~30 chip and hardware stocks, re-runs the backtest, and on the first trading day of Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec records the top picks into the paper portfolio.

      Data health

      Is the data behind each score complete, fresh and sane? Numbers are quarters of SEC data available per series. ⚠ rows need a look before you trust that stock's score.

      Run health

      The last 10 automatic updates. A failed run leaves the previous day's numbers in place and opens a GitHub issue, so you get a phone notification.

      Honest caveats

      • Survivorship bias: the universe is today's companies, so the backtest never held a chip stock that later collapsed or got acquired. Expect real results to be worse.
      • Overfitting: if you tweak factor weights until the backtest looks great, it will look great — and tell you nothing. Change rules rarely, and judge them by the paper record.
      • Point-in-time: fundamentals only count from the date they were filed with the SEC, so the backtest can't peek at future reports.
      • Foreign filers (ASML, TSMC) file annually under IFRS, so they're scored on price factors only.
      • Costs of 10 bps per trade are included; taxes aren't. This is a research tool, not financial advice.
